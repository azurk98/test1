Sarajevo Population Grid

Estimated residents in every hexagonal cell of Centar, Stari Grad, Novo Sarajevo and Novi Grad. Each municipality adds up exactly to the official FZS figure; everything below that level is a model allocation from OSM buildings and WorldPop, so each cell also carries a range and a reliability grade.

  • GradesA: the cell's MZ total is constrained (Stari Grad from the MZ figures in its 2024–2027 strategy; Novi Grad from 2010 MZ records blended 50/50 with the model). B: only the municipality total is known. C: no mapped buildings, so WorldPop alone places people.
  • RangeMin and max across nine runs: building weight α = 0.5 / 0.7 / 0.9 against WorldPop, and estimated floor counts −1 / 0 / +1. The main estimate uses α per municipality: 0.9 in Novi Grad and Novo Sarajevo, 0.5 in Stari Grad, 0.7 in Centar.
  • CalibrationIn Novi Grad and Novo Sarajevo a square metre of house counts half as much as one in a block of 5+ floors, and WorldPop gets 10% of the weight, fitted to Novi Grad's 2010 MZ records. Stari Grad's old neighbourhoods fit best with no house correction and an even split with WorldPop: the MZ shares then differ from the municipality's own figures by 16% on average (30% with Novi Grad's settings). Centar has no MZ data and takes the midpoint.
  • Floor countsOnly 4.7% of OSM buildings record their floors. The rest come from a model trained on those that do (spatial cross-check: r = 0.70), since GHSL heights alone barely separate houses from towers (r = 0.13).
  • AttractivenessA location score from 0 to 100, separate from the population estimate. It weighs, as percentile ranks among populated cells: residents within a 10-minute walk (20%) and a 10-minute drive (15%), shops and services within a 10-minute walk (20%), stops within 400 m (15%), distance to the nearest stop (10%), distance to a main road (10%) and intersection density (10%). Walking and driving times run on the OSM street network. Walking slows on slopes (Tobler's hiking function: 4.8 km/h on the flat, 3.4 km/h up a 10% grade); driving assumes 65% of the speed limit, and transit counts stops only, without timetables. Cells at the edge toward Ilidža and Vogošća under-count reachable residents, since those municipalities are not modelled.
  • LivabilityWalking minutes, with slopes, from each cell to the nearest grocery, transit stop, primary school, clinic, park, kindergarten, pharmacy, playground, hospital and sports ground. Each counts half at a set time (5 min for a stop, 8 for a shop or park, 12 for a school or clinic, 25 for a hospital) and is weighted: grocery, transit and school 15% each, clinic and park 12%, kindergarten 8%, pharmacy 7%, playground 6%, hospital and sports 5%. Points come off near a motorway or primary road (up to 10), in industrial areas (10) and on very steep ground (5). Weights are a judgement, not a calibration. Places come from OpenStreetMap.
  • TransitRoutes of all tram and trolleybus lines and part of the bus network come from OpenStreetMap. Each cell gets a Public Transport Accessibility Level (PTAL, the Transport for London method): walking time to stops (8 min for bus and trolleybus, 12 for tram, with slopes), plus half the interval as waiting time, with each line counted once at its best stop. There is no published timetable data for Sarajevo, so departures per hour are assumptions by line type (tram 3: 10 an hour, other trams 5, main trolleybuses 6, others 4, buses 3, stops without a mapped line 2) kept in data/linije_frekvencija.csv until real timetables replace them.
  • Best to liveOne score that mixes every layer, weighted by who is choosing: public transport (PTAL), daily needs within walking distance (the profile sets which needs count most, such as school and kindergarten for families or clinic and pharmacy for seniors), car access, winter sun (3 h or less scores 0, 7.8 h scores 100), clean winter air and little fog (from the smog and fog indices), distance from main-road noise and industry, and gentle terrain (5% slope or less scores 100, 25% scores 0). Only cells with residents are scored. Scores are ranks within the city: each factor is first turned into its rank among populated cells (0 = worst, 100 = best), the ranks are weighted, and the result is ranked again, so a score of 87 means the cell beats 87% of populated cells for that profile. This spreads scores across the full range instead of bunching them near the middle. Move the sliders to set your own weights.
  • Smog and fogWinter smog and fog in Sarajevo come from the temperature inversion that traps cold air and smoke in the valley. Each cell's exposure is the share of inversion days it lies below the inversion top, from its height above the valley floor (lowest ground within 3 km) assuming a typical top about 200 m above the floor, raised near busy roads (smog) or rivers (fog). These are relative indices, not measurements. They agree in direction with the FHMZ stations shown on the map (2025 annual PM2.5: Bjelave 26, Otoka 35, Saraj Polje 61, Ilidža 29, Vogošća 26 µg/m³; station positions approximate), but five stations cannot pin down street-level values, and the biggest local factor, gas versus wood or coal heating, has no open data.
  • Winter sunHours of direct sun on 21 December and 21 January on a clear day. For seven points per cell the sun's path is traced every 5 minutes against the horizon formed by the surrounding mountains (Trebević, Igman, Hum and others, up to 25 km away, with the Earth's curvature) from the Copernicus surface model with buildings filtered out. Shade from neighbouring buildings and the valley's winter fog are not included. On a flat horizon the day would give 8.8 hours.
  • Car accessFor each cell, the drive along OSM streets from its centre to the nearest tertiary or larger road: the steepest 100 m on the way, metres of narrow road, and the share of the cell's own streets that are steep (over 12%) or narrow. OSM almost never records street width, so width is estimated as the gap between buildings on both sides; fences and walls are not mapped, so real streets can be narrower. Gradients come from the Copernicus surface model with buildings filtered out, measured over 100 m. Poor access takes up to 15 points off livability.
  • MZ outlinesNovi Grad's MZ outlines come from the official street list: buildings are matched by address, or by the nearest named street when that whole street belongs to one MZ (70% of homes), and the rest by their neighbours. Novo Sarajevo and Stari Grad outlines come from OpenStreetMap.

Supermarket sites

A Huff model splits each cell's grocery trips over the supermarkets within 20 minutes, weighted by store size and by travel time squared (walking with slopes taken into account, or driving plus two minutes to park). A new standard supermarket is placed in every cell that has none and lies within 400 m of a main road, and sites are picked one at a time so later picks compete with earlier ones. Customer figures are residents, not sales.

Car wash sites

A car wash lives on two kinds of customer: residents who drive to it, and drivers who stop on the way. Residents are shared over the washes within a 12-minute drive by a Huff model (travel time squared, a fuel station's wash counts 0.6, a detailing studio 0.4), assuming 0.35 cars per resident and one paid wash per car a month. Passing trade assumes 0.1% of the road's typical daily traffic stops, split with every wash within 1 km. Candidate cells sit on a tertiary or bigger road, have slopes of 8% or less, no narrow or steep access, buildings on under 30% of the ground, and no wash yet.

Delivery kitchen sites

A delivery-only kitchen needs no shop window: what counts is how many adults its couriers reach and how many restaurants already deliver there. Adults aged 15–64 (and 65+ at a fifth) are shared over every restaurant and fast-food place within a 15-minute drive, with distance mattering less than for a shop (travel time to the power 1). A new kitchen running several brands counts double a fast-food place, a sit-down restaurant half. Orders assume one delivery per adult a month. Sites sit within 100 m of a tertiary or bigger road for couriers, on slopes of 10% or less, with easy street access.

Music school sites

Pupils are children aged about 7 to 18, estimated as 55% of under-15s plus 4% of 15–64-year-olds, of whom 5% take music lessons. They reach a school on foot (with slopes) or are driven (plus three minutes to park), whichever is quicker, up to 20 minutes. Public music schools count three times a private one, since they are free and larger. Sites need a transit stop within 400 m or a road within 150 m, slopes of 12% or less, easy street access, and existing buildings to rent space in.
